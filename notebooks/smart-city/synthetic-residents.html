Module 11 · before you watch

The test behind the story

A vendor tells the city: skip the survey, describe each resident to an AI and let the AI answer for them. We tested that pitch on 300 real people whose answers we already know.

1 · The survey

The General Social Survey (GSS) is run by NORC at the University of Chicago. It has asked a national sample of U.S. adults the same core questions since 1972. We used the 2024 survey: 3,986 people. One block of questions is about government spending. The interviewer reads:

“We are faced with many problems in this country, none of which can be solved easily or inexpensively. I'm going to name some of these problems, and for each one I'd like you to tell me whether you think we're spending too much money on it, too little money, or about the right amount.”

Then the topics, one by one, and the person answers too little, about right or too much. We used five topics.

Two wordings, chosen at random. For three topics the GSS has two ways of naming the same spending, and each person hears only one of them, picked at random (a split ballot). Because the split is random, the two halves are alike in every other way, so any difference in their answers is caused by the words alone.

Read it: on highways and bridges the real people split about half “too little”, the rest mostly “about right”. Change two words and answers move: “solving the problems of the big cities” gets 54% “too little”, “assistance to big cities” only 21%. For the environment the two wordings mean the same thing, and people answered the same way (64% and 64%).

3 · The people we used

Of the 2024 respondents, 3,275 answered all five topics and every background question. We drew 300 at random (fixed seed 2372, so anyone can redraw the same people): 137 Democrats (including independents who lean Democrat), 62 independents, 97 Republicans (including leaners) and 4 from other parties. Results are unweighted: the AI is compared with these same 300 people, not with the whole country.

Two models, gpt-5.4-mini and gpt-6.1-sol, each asked three separate times with the identical prompt: 1,800 recorded answers (Oct 7, 2026). This follows the published “silicon sampling” design (Argyle et al., Political Analysis, 2023).

Limits: demographics only (no interview); the instruction asks for the person's “most likely” answer, which itself pulls toward the typical answer; groups are small and unweighted; one survey, two models, one date. Published work finds the same pattern (Bisbee et al., Political Analysis, 2024; Wang, Morgenstern & Dickerson, 2025).
